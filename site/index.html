Abstract

This project examines differences in metabolic and demographic characteristics by recorded diabetes diagnosis in the historical Pima Indians Diabetes dataset. The workflow identifies zero placeholders in physiological measurements, applies complete-case filtering, and compares the retained diagnostic groups.

Of 768 source records, 392 had complete measurements across the five selected fields. Mean glucose was 145.2 mg/dL in the diabetes group and 111.4 mg/dL in the no-diabetes group, a difference of 33.8 mg/dL (Welch’s t = 11.15; p < 0.0001; Cohen’s d = 1.28). Recorded diabetes was also associated with age group (χ² = 33.40; p < 0.0001). These findings describe the complete-case sample and must be interpreted alongside substantial missingness and the cohort’s restricted demographic composition.

Keywords   Diabetes mellitus · Missing data · Welch’s t-test · Chi-square · Effect size

Study objective

What differs between the diagnostic groups?

The analysis asks whether average glucose differs between records with and without a recorded diabetes diagnosis, and whether diagnosis is associated with age group. BMI and age are summarized descriptively to provide context. A separate data-quality assessment documents missing measurements before any statistical comparison.

Methods

From source records to an analytical sample

The source file contains 768 observations and nine variables. The historical cohort consists of Pima Indian women aged 21 years and older. The notebook checks the expected dataset shape and exact duplicate rows, then treats zeros in glucose, blood pressure, skin thickness, insulin, and BMI as missing measurements. A value of zero for pregnancies remains valid.

Statistical approach

Continuous comparison
A two-sided Welch’s independent-samples t-test compares glucose means without assuming equal population variances.
Categorical association
A chi-square test of independence compares age group (<40 versus ≥40 years) and diagnosis, with SciPy’s default continuity correction.
Magnitude of difference
Cohen’s d expresses the glucose mean difference relative to the pooled sample standard deviation.

The analysis uses an exploratory significance threshold of 0.05. BMI and age means are descriptive; the notebook does not conduct a BMI hypothesis test or fit a predictive model.

Results

Glucose and age differ by recorded diagnosis

The complete-case sample includes 262 records without diabetes and 130 with diabetes. The diabetes group had higher mean glucose and BMI and was older on average.

Glucose comparison

Mean glucose was 33.8 mg/dL higher in the diabetes group. Welch’s t-test provided evidence against equal group means (t = 11.15; p < 0.0001). Cohen’s d was 1.28, indicating a substantial standardized difference in this sample.

Age-group association

Among records under age 40, 83 of 316 (26.3%) had a diabetes diagnosis. Among those aged 40 or older, 47 of 76 (61.8%) had a diagnosis. The chi-square test indicated an association between age group and recorded diagnosis (χ² = 33.40; 1 degree of freedom; p < 0.0001).

Discussion

Interpreting the findings

The glucose comparison shows an expected association with a known diagnostic marker. It should not be presented as a novel predictor of diabetes. The age-group comparison also describes an unadjusted association; it does not establish an independent age effect or account for other characteristics.

A small p-value is evidence against the specified null model under its assumptions, not the probability that the observed result occurred “by chance.” Cohen’s d describes the standardized magnitude of the difference, but does not by itself establish clinical importance.

Limitations

  • Complete-case selection. Excluding 376 records may introduce selection bias if measurement availability is related to diagnosis or other characteristics.
  • Population scope. Findings from this historical cohort of Pima Indian women cannot be assumed to generalize to other populations.
  • Observational design. The analysis cannot establish temporal or causal relationships.
  • Exploratory comparisons. No confounder adjustment, sensitivity analysis of missingness, or external validation is included.

Conclusion

In the complete-case sample, recorded diabetes was associated with higher mean glucose and with age group. The project demonstrates a workflow for auditing clinical data, handling zero placeholders, comparing groups, and reporting effect size. A useful next step would be to examine how complete and incomplete records differ before choosing a strategy for handling missing data.
